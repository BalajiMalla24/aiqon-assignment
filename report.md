# Predicting Shipment ETA and Delay Propagation

**AIQON take-home assignment | Technical report**

## 1. Problem and forecast checkpoint

The system forecasts a shipment's final arrival after it has cleared customs at its destination port. This checkpoint makes the forecast operationally clear: departure, ocean transit, port processing, and customs observations are available, while the final delivery outcome is still unknown. The predicted ETA is the scheduled final arrival plus the predicted delay.

Delay is measured in hours as **actual final arrival minus scheduled final arrival**. A shipment is classified as late when that delay exceeds **24 hours**. Using a fixed threshold turns the probability output into an interpretable planning signal without implying that all smaller delays are harmless.

## 2. Synthetic journeys and assumptions

The reproducible dataset contains 240 shipments on four named trade lanes. Each record includes origin and destination, scheduled and actual departure, port arrival, customs clearance and final-arrival timestamps, stage delays, port processing time, a congestion index, and weather severity. The generator uses seed 42 by default.

The simulation makes delays propagate through the journey. Departure and sailing delays, port waiting time, processing deviation, and customs delay accumulate into the observed schedule deviation at the forecast checkpoint. The final delay adds a downstream adjustment influenced by congestion, weather exposure for the route, and the congestion-by-processing interaction, plus random variation. Actual timestamps are constructed from the scheduled timestamps and these stage increments, so the final-delay label is verifiable directly from the arrival timestamps.

These are explicit modeling assumptions, not claims about measured carrier behavior. Routes have fixed scheduled transit durations and route-specific weather exposure. Congestion and weather are assumed to be available at the customs-clearance checkpoint; a real deployment must source only observations or forecasts available at that time. The synthetic data has no missing events, cancellations, transshipment, or external data feed. Random noise represents unobserved operational variation.

## 3. Features, models, and explanations

The engineered inputs include stage delays observed by customs clearance, total checkpoint delay, port pressure (waiting plus processing), route, departure weekday and month, congestion, and weather. Two interaction features capture relationships that isolated stage values miss: congestion multiplied by processing time, and weather multiplied by route exposure.

Ridge regression estimates final delay in hours. A logistic regression estimates the probability that delay exceeds 24 hours. Both models use standardized features and are implemented with Python's standard library: the small regression solve uses regularized normal equations, and logistic weights are fit with gradient descent. This keeps the code auditable and avoids a large runtime stack for a small dataset.

For each prediction, the report lists signed regression contributions: each fitted coefficient multiplied by that feature's standardized distance from its training mean. Positive values push the prediction later; negative values push it earlier. Correlated inputs can share or shift these contributions, so they explain the model calculation rather than establish causal effects.

## 5. Interpretation and next steps

The prediction combines the observed accumulated delay with estimated downstream risk. Congestion can affect the port queue and its interaction with processing duration; weather effects vary by route exposure. The explanation output makes these influences inspectable for an individual shipment, while the late probability communicates uncertainty near the 24-hour decision boundary.

The reported test metrics should not be read as expected real-world performance. Training and test records share the same synthetic rules, routes, and time range, and the generator is deliberately simpler than maritime operations. The sample is also too small to support robust estimates across individual routes. There is no separate external validation set, and the logistic probability has not been calibrated on real outcomes.

With historical data, the next steps would be to validate using a forward-in-time split, report error by route and delay band, test missing or late-arriving checkpoint data, and calibrate late probabilities. Operational use would also require monitoring schedule changes and model drift. The current solution is a focused, reproducible baseline that demonstrates stage-aware features, delay propagation, ETA estimation, and local explanations without claiming that synthetic-data fit transfers to production.
